From One Report to Two Histories: Building an Agent for Historical Newspaper Research

A reproducible workflow for finding, verifying, and comparing multilingual newspaper evidence without allowing AI to replace the historian’s judgment.

On July 11, 1938, near the village of Dabburiya, a force of British soldiers and Jewish auxiliary policemen encountered an armed Arab group. The battle appeared the next day in Hebrew and Arabic newspapers. Several basic details recurred: at least three members of the armed group were killed, one Jewish auxiliary policeman was killed, and Captain Orde Wingate and several soldiers and policemen were wounded. The readers did not, however, receive the same story.

Haaretz placed the report on its front page within a cluster titled “A Wave of Terror throughout the Country.” Davar published a short item on the same page under the heading “Battle with a Large Gang,” alongside a report on the funeral of auxiliary policeman Yosef Ben-Moshe, and expanded its account in the evening supplement. The Arabic newspaper Filasṭīn included a “Description of the Battle of Dabburiya” under a broad headline about casualties and violence. Its report continued from the battle to what followed: a large force entered the village, surrounded it, and searched the houses. Three newspapers reported one event. Each selected a different point of observation.1

When I began searching for the Arabic-press counterpart to the Haganah Field Companies and the Special Night Squads, I first searched for the names of the units. It was a logical starting point, and it produced almost nothing. The equivalent Arabic phrases did not appear in the relevant results. Wingate’s name appeared only after I tried several spellings. The search changed when I stopped asking what the unit was called and began asking what had happened: where the event took place, when it occurred, who participated, who was hurt, and what the force did after the battle. A search for Dabburiya returned eighteen results for 1938. Two led to this event.2

This is where the agent emerged. I did not ask it to write history for me. I asked it to remember every query, suggest alternatives across Hebrew and Arabic, separate OCR from what appears on the page, and link reports only when the date, place, and at least two additional details matched. Dabburiya became more than a search term. It became a test of method. The question was no longer only what each newspaper had reported. It was also how the route from archive to historical claim could be reconstructed, inspected, and corrected.

1. From Scarce Access to Too Much Information

Before the information revolution, scarcity began before the reading did. A researcher had to know where a newspaper was held, travel to a library or archive, request bound volumes or scroll through microfilm, photograph or scan the relevant issues, and find a way to return to them later. Even when microfilm removed the need to visit the original archive, the work remained sequential: page after page. OCR databases and digital photography changed that order. Researchers can now search centuries of newspapers and thousands of pages within minutes. They can sometimes find information without knowing in advance which collection contains it.3

Access became easier, but the bottleneck moved. Careful research still requires many hours. The abundance of sources requires the researcher to choose queries, distinguish accidental matches from relevant reports, check OCR against the page, preserve context, and determine whether two accounts rely on the same communiqué. A digital newspaper archive is not a transparent copy of the press. It is a selective database shaped by title selection, scan quality, search design, and errors in character recognition. Studies of digital newspaper collections show that these errors can cause a substantial share of relevant mentions to disappear from search results.4

This is where artificial intelligence can help keep us from drowning in information. An agent can already propose multilingual variants, locate sources, search within them, sort candidates, import files at the highest quality the archive permits, preserve metadata and links, compare accounts, and identify agreements and contradictions. In time, it may read multilingual pages more accurately and connect distant versions of the same event. It does not determine scan quality or grant permission to reuse an image. Those decisions remain with the archive and the rights holders. Nor does it replace reading. Its contribution is to narrow the space in which human reading must be exact.

2. From Five Questions to an Event Reconstruction

I did not try to scan the entire archive. I began with a Hebrew-language pilot organized around five questions. The first asked how the press described Wingate at the time. The second examined the Hebrew names used for the Special Night Squads. The third concerned the relationship between Wingate and Yitzhak Sadeh. The final two asked when the terms Nodedot and Fosh appeared, and how the transition from passive defense to offensive action was described. The boundaries made it possible to test which query families worked, where the OCR misled, and what documentation another researcher would need to reconstruct the route.

In the Arabic-press pilot, I again began by translating unit names. The queries الفرق الليلية and فصائل الميدان returned no matches. Three other spellings of Wingate’s name also produced nothing. The search log preserved these failures. I then expanded the matrix to include names and alternate spellings, places such as Hanita and Dabburiya, infrastructure such as the oil pipeline, and actions and outcomes such as patrol, ambush, shooting, casualties, encirclement, and search. The spelling ونجيت produced two results. The phrase الكابتن ونجيت led directly to the Dabburiya report.5

Once the reports had been found, I did not connect them because they shared a word. Two sources were defined as describing the same event only when the date and place overlapped and at least two further features matched. In Dabburiya, these were the mixed British-Jewish force, Wingate’s injury, the casualty balance, and the operation in the village after the battle. The same rule left other links at “probable” when one decisive detail remained missing. The event became the unit of comparison. The keyword remained a navigational aid.

3. Between the Page and the Claim

The search produced candidates, not evidence. For every record, I preserved a chain of six layers. The first is the metadata supplied by the archive: newspaper, date, and link. The second is the raw OCR produced by the machine. The third is a transcription I read from the scan. The fourth is a cautious normalization of a misspelled title or historical spelling. The fifth is translation. Only the sixth layer contains research inference. These are not six competing versions of one text. They are separate stations along the route from page to claim. The distinction shows what the archive supplied, what the machine guessed, what the researcher read, and what the researcher concluded.

The archive itself requires the same transparency. A digital collection is a selection from the newspapers that survived, and a result list is a further selection produced by the query and the quality of the OCR. Research on collection bias, interdisciplinary workflows, and the impresso interface converges on one requirement: expose the collection’s composition, the provenance of the data, the stages of processing, and the quality of each stage.6

Dabburiya exposed another problem. Haaretz attributed the battle details to the Government Information Office. Similar details and casualty figures in Davar make a shared official source plausible, although not fully proved. Filasṭīn combined the core of an official announcement with additional information about the encirclement of the village and the house searches. The three publications therefore produced three frames, but not necessarily three independent accounts. I counted publications, information origins, and points of view separately. The same communiqué is not a third confirmation of the facts. It is evidence of how each newsroom chose to position, expand, and frame them.7

4. From Pilot to Skill

After the pilot, I turned the sequence into a skill: a reusable package of instructions and templates that can be applied to a different question. The input is limited to a focused question, a date range, languages, and perspectives. The agent then constructs a search matrix, runs variants, and records every query in a log, including zero-result searches. Each candidate receives an identifier and enters a source manifest, a documented inventory of articles, links, verification states, and rights limitations. An event crosswalk tests which reports describe the same case and how dependent they are on one another. Only then does a candidate reach the human approval gate.

The language model enters this process as an assistant, not as an authoritative copy of the newspaper. It can propose OCR corrections, but it must not erase the raw output or replace the scan. The reason is empirical, not merely theoretical. In a study of nineteenth-century Latin American Spanish newspapers, about 78 percent of the changes proposed by the model were classified as hallucinations, while only about 12 percent were actual OCR corrections. The finding belongs to the setting tested in that study, but the wider warning travels well: a machine correction is a hypothesis that must be checked.8

The same rule applies to coding. The model can suggest whether a report relies on a communiqué, a local correspondent, or editorial synthesis, and can help rank candidates by relevance. The researcher must define the categories and test them against a human-coded sample. Recent work on qualitative coding illustrates this order. ASReview, a tool that uses active learning to screen scholarly literature, offers a complementary analogy: the algorithm prioritizes records, while the researcher decides what to include and when to stop. The skill does not fully implement either method. It adopts their division of labor. The machine shortens the queue and preserves the trail of decisions. The researcher verifies, interprets, and cites.9

5. What the Method Adds

The contribution is not a new algorithm. It is a lightweight and accessible connection, designed for an individual researcher, among practices already familiar to several research communities: multilingual search, criticism of collection boundaries, candidate prioritization, model-assisted coding, and source verification. The connection is organized around a historical event and produces an evidence package that can be inspected, transferred to another researcher, and corrected. It is especially useful when opposing groups described the same action in different terms.

The method has limits. The digital archive is not the entire press. OCR may conceal a relevant report. Knowledge of Arabic and Hebrew remains essential for checking names, context, and meaning. A source that has been found and verified may still be dependent on other reports. Access and rights restrictions determine what can be retained and what can be published. For these reasons, the skill does not claim exhaustiveness. It leaves gaps visible and records which next check might reduce them.

In the Dabburiya case, the agent did not resolve the historical question. It made the path to the answer visible, open to inspection, and open to correction.
